A Computational Swinging Lexical Network

We implement the Swinging Lexical Network model of word production computationally, letting activation spread through a 30,000-word distributional semantic network. Across 392,156 picture-word interference trials from 86 English and German experiments, one semantic predictor holds in both languages: naming slows when a few co-activated words stand out as strong competitors, and speeds up when activation is spread thinly across many.

Louis Schiekiera · Rasha Abdel Rahman · Alissa Melinger · Vincent Gruber · Fritz Günther

October 15, 2026 · psycholinguistics language-production spreading-activation distributional-semantics

Paper Data

Motivation

In picture-word interference (PWI), speakers name a picture (a dog) while ignoring a distractor word (CAT). Related distractors usually slow naming down. The Swinging Lexical Network (SLN) model explains this through the lexical cohort that target and distractor jointly activate (for dog-CAT, words like mouse, horse or pet): priming within the cohort speeds naming up, competition within it slows naming down.

As a verbal theory, the SLN leaves one question open: what counts as a competitor? Any co-activated word, or only words whose activation stands out from the rest? Building the model computationally forced us to be explicit in our modelling choices.

The model

We let activation spread through a 30,000-word fastText network (separately for English and German), starting from the target and the distractor. From this process we derive two measures:

Cohort concentration

The entropy of the joint activation after one spreading step. Low entropy: a few words absorb most of the activation. High entropy: activation is spread thinly over many words.

The swing

How much the activation pattern still changes over later spreading steps, measured as the KL divergence from the network's stationary distribution.

Cohort concentration. The target and distractor rows of the one-step transition matrix are averaged over their neighbors, giving the joint-activation distribution. Its entropy is high when activation is spread evenly (left) and low when a few neighbors dominate (right).

We tested these measures in a chain of six mixed-effects models, each adding one semantic construct (cosine similarity, neighborhood density, directional activation, cohort concentration, swing), on the harmonized PWI dataset: 392,156 trials from 86 experiments.

Results

Cohort concentration was the only semantic predictor with a reliable effect in both languages. Naming was slower when the cohort was concentrated and faster when activation was diffuse.

This decides the open question in favor of an effective-competitor reading. Total activation is fixed, so a flat distribution means that no word stands out: it marks the absence of competition, not a maximal cohort. Competitors are the words whose activation rises above that background.

Two readings of cohort entropy. Under the effective-competitor interpretation (middle), only neighbors with activation above the near-uniform background compete, so a flat profile yields almost no competition. Under the nominal-competitor interpretation (bottom), every co-activated neighbor counts. The data support the effective-competitor reading.

The remaining predictors did less:

  • Cosine similarity had no stable population effect once cohort concentration entered the model.
  • Neighborhood density of the target or distractor alone had no stable effect either: in PWI, what matters seems to be the cohort of the pair, not each word separately.
  • The swing improved fit, but could not be separated from cohort concentration (within-experiment r = −0.84 in English, −0.97 in German). Spreading converges on the same network-wide state for every word pair, so later steps mostly re-express the first-step cohort.

Takeaway

In the SLN, semantic interference depends on how co-activation is distributed across the cohort, and a competitor is a word that stands out, not every word that receives some activation.
